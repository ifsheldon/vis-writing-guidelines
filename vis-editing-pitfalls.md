# VIS Editing Pitfalls

Polishing an existing manuscript should make its argument easier to follow while preserving its intended meaning and evidential basis.
A fluent replacement can still change the comparison, omit a consequence, or disrupt the explanation.
Check those relationships before optimizing individual sentences.

Use this guide alongside [VIS Writing Style](vis-writing-style.md), [VIS Writing Pitfalls](vis-writing-pitfalls.md), and [VIS Writing Guide](vis-writing-guideline.md).
This guide focuses on mistakes introduced while editing an existing passage.
The examples are illustrative; claims used in a manuscript must match its evidence.

## 1. Improving a sentence while changing the argument

**Pitfall:** The replacement sounds clearer but answers a different question, changes the relationship between observations, or loses part of the author's intended claim.

Before rewriting, describe what the passage establishes in plain language.
For example, a paragraph might establish an apparent balance, examine its underlying distribution, and explain two distinct limitations.
An edit that retains only the limitations loses the reason for examining the distribution.
An edit that retains only one limitation changes the conclusion.

**Better approach:** Identify the observations, interpretations, and consequences that the passage needs to preserve.
Keep the opening overview when it prepares readers to interpret the details.
Check both the preceding and following sentences, including references such as “this,” “these patterns,” and “therefore.”
Verify that a transition expresses the same relationship after the edit.

If the intended reasoning is unclear, reread the original passage and surrounding section before producing another draft.
Clarify unresolved meaning with the author instead of repeatedly polishing an assumed argument.
When the evidence requires a substantive change to the claim, explain that change explicitly.

## 2. Manufacturing symmetry for a smoother comparison

**Pitfall:** A parallel construction imposes a contrast that the evidence does not establish.

Suppose a survey finds that research prototypes cover a narrow set of tasks and that deployed products use a narrow set of representations.
The sentence below changes those observations:

> Research prototypes emphasize exploration, while deployed products emphasize practical work.

It invents a distinction between purposes and loses the observation about representations.

**Better approach:** Preserve the dimension on which each observation was made.
The two findings can be stated separately:

> Research prototypes cover a narrow set of tasks. Many deployed products rely on a small set of conventional representations.

Use parallel phrasing when the claims are genuinely comparable.
Similar sentence structures do not justify attributing different goals, audiences, or capabilities to the groups.

## 3. Replacing consequences with generic implications

**Pitfall:** The ending names a gap or recommends improvement without explaining why the observation matters.

Phrases such as “the approaches remain disconnected” or “this creates opportunities for integration” can repeat the problem without developing it.

Consider two views of the same records that use different identifiers without showing their correspondence.
A concrete consequence is:

> Users must reconstruct which entries refer to the same record before comparing the views.

This identifies the affected user, the additional work, and the task it obstructs.

**Better approach:** Ask what support is missing, what action becomes difficult, or what information remains unavailable.
Preserve distinct consequences when an observation affects different groups or tasks in different ways.
State only consequences supported by the evidence or by an explained relationship.
Do not invent user difficulties to give a paragraph a stronger ending.
If no useful implication follows, ending with the observation is acceptable.

## 4. Adding concrete details that belong elsewhere

**Pitfall:** An example is accurate and specific but changes the paragraph's level of analysis.

A paragraph comparing coverage across research areas may not be the right place to describe how users switch between tutorials and development tools.
That example concerns a particular workflow and may belong in the later application analysis.

**Better approach:** Identify the paragraph's job before adding detail: introducing a finding, explaining a mechanism, comparing evidence, or examining an application.
Retain details that advance that job and that the reader has enough context to understand.
Move details that begin a different explanation to the place where that explanation is developed.

When an example requires substantial new background, check whether it is premature.
Specificity alone does not make a detail relevant.

## 5. Letting measurement details displace the main message

**Pitfall:** A figure introduction describes counting or encoding operations before explaining what the figure helps readers understand.

**Mechanical emphasis:**

> Figure 4 displays assignment counts in a task-by-subject matrix.

**Purpose followed by measurement:**

> Figure 4 shows support coverage across tasks and subjects. Coverage in each cell is measured by the number of item assignments.

The second version gives the measurement a purpose.

**Better approach:** Lead with the phenomenon, comparison, or support pattern the figure communicates.
Then explain the counting unit or encoding needed to interpret it.
Keep captions understandable on their own, and briefly introduce unfamiliar measurement terms where the main text first relies on them.
Avoid repeating the entire caption in the body.

## 6. Changing claim strength before inspecting the evidence

**Pitfall:** An editor weakens a claim because its support is not visible in the sentence, or strengthens it because a plausible explanation sounds persuasive.

Relevant support may already appear in a table's coding, a figure legend, an operational definition, or a later analysis.
For example, a table may explicitly distinguish conventional representations from tailored designs.
That distinction can support a statement about representation use even if the surrounding paragraph discusses only totals.

**Better approach:** Inspect the relevant evidence and definitions before changing the claim.
If support exists, connect the statement to it explicitly.
If support is partial, narrow the statement to the population, category, or condition actually examined.

Check scope as well as presence.
A code for one feature does not characterize every feature of a tool.
A distribution of publications can establish an emphasis; a claim about effects on users needs an additional basis.
Keep observations and interpretations distinguishable without adding generic assurances about being evidence-based.

## 7. Preserving numbers while changing what they count

**Pitfall:** The numerals remain unchanged, but the surrounding nouns or denominator give them a different meaning.

In a matrix of subjects and purposes, an item with one subject label and one purpose label belongs to one cell.
It has two labels but contributes one cell assignment.
An item covering two subjects for one purpose contributes two assignments.
Summing cells can therefore count the same item more than once.

Calling a sum “half of the tools” when it is half of all assignments changes the finding.
Likewise, a group's share of a category does not establish that every item in that category has a particular design property.

**Better approach:** Identify the counted entity and denominator before rephrasing a quantitative statement.
Distinguish individual labels, memberships in combinations of categories, and distinct items.
Use wording that matches the calculation, such as “half of all assignments.”
Check consistency across the figure, caption, analysis, and introduction.
When sources disagree, investigate the discrepancy before replacing one set of numbers with another.

## 8. Filling gaps with bland observations or defensive qualifications

**Pitfall:** An added sentence sounds cautious or insightful but contributes no new explanation.

An observation that two groups overlap may add little after the overlap has already been quantified.
A sentence saying that proposed research directions are not exhaustive may add little when the passage already presents them as the authors' recommendations.

**Better approach:** Ask what the sentence changes in the reader's understanding.
Does it establish a condition, explain a relationship, identify a consequence, or delimit a claim in a consequential way?
If it does none of these, remove it.

Integrate necessary qualifications into the relevant claim when possible:

> Based on the survey, we propose three research directions that we consider promising.

This identifies the basis and status of the proposal without a separate defensive sentence.
Retain uncertainty and limitations that affect what readers can conclude or do.
Do not manufacture an insight merely to avoid a short paragraph or a direct ending.

## A practical editing pass

1. **Recover the intended message.** State what the passage should establish, including the relationships and consequences that must survive.
2. **Read its context.** Identify what readers already know and what the next sentence or paragraph needs from this one.
3. **Check its basis.** Inspect relevant evidence, definitions, units, and denominators before changing claim strength or numerical wording.
4. **Draft within that scope.** Improve wording while preserving the argument; explain any necessary substantive change separately.
5. **Compare meanings.** Check actors, comparison dimensions, scope, causal language, and consequences against the original and the evidence.
6. **Read the final passage alone.** Confirm that its explanation works without the editing conversation, and check connected summaries and captions for consistency.

When an author says that a draft sounds good by itself but changes the logic, stop adjusting individual phrases and return to the original argument.
